Seattle's Emergency Calls Tell a Bigger Story

What 911 call data from 2020 and 2025 reveals about healthcare, mental health, and what public safety actually looks like.

Why this matters When most people think of 911, they picture violent crime. But Seattle's call data tells a different story — one where mental health crises, welfare checks, and disturbances account for far more emergency responses than shootings or assaults. Understanding what people actually call 911 for is the first step toward building a public safety system that can actually meet those needs.

In 2020, Seattle was at a flashpoint. The COVID-19 pandemic reshaped daily life, and the nationwide response to George Floyd's death brought urgent questions about what public safety should look like. Five years later, those debates are still unresolved — but the data has continued to accumulate.

This project explores 911 call records from both years to ask: what kinds of emergencies are people actually experiencing? Where do they happen? And how have those patterns changed? The data shows that emergency response in Seattle is deeply tied to housing, healthcare access, and mental health — not just policing.

Section 1 — Call Distribution

Medical and wellness-related calls make up a much larger portion of Seattle's emergency response than most people expect

The top 12 call categories in 2025 — ranked by volume. Hover over any bar to see the exact count. Violent crime categories sit far down the list.

So what? The categories dominating Seattle's 911 log — suspicious circumstances, disturbances, welfare checks — are situations where a police response may not always be the best fit. Cities that have added mental health co-responders and community navigators to their emergency systems have seen reduced repeat calls for these same categories.

Context: The pandemic years brought major shifts in how people interacted with emergency services — lockdowns, economic stress, and disrupted mental health care all affected call volumes. Some of those shifts have persisted well into 2025.
Section 3 — Biggest Changes Since 2020

Some call types grew significantly — others dropped. The changes point to real shifts in community need.

Green bars show call types that increased from 2020 to 2025; red bars show those that decreased. The length of each bar shows the size of the change.

So what? Sustained increases in certain call categories are a signal — not just of more crime or disorder, but often of unmet need. An uptick in welfare check calls, for example, may reflect gaps in mental health services rather than a spike in danger.

Section 5 — Different Neighborhoods, Different Emergencies

Different Seattle precincts experience very different emergency response demand

Seattle's five precincts — North, East, South, West, and Southwest — each cover distinct neighborhoods with different demographics, housing conditions, and access to community resources. Emergency response demand is not spread evenly across the city.

Population density, healthcare access, housing stability, and the presence (or absence) of social services all shape how often and why residents call 911. The West Precinct, which covers downtown and surrounding areas, has historically handled a high share of crisis-related calls.

Comparing how each precinct's call volume changed between 2020 and 2025 helps show where community needs may be growing — and where investment in non-police support services might make the biggest difference.

Takeaway Seattle's 911 data makes one thing clear: public safety is about much more than policing alone. The calls coming in every day reflect a city navigating overlapping crises — housing, mental health, substance use, and poverty. A public safety system built only around a police response will keep getting called to situations it wasn't designed to handle.

The data also shows that these patterns are not static. COVID-19 shifted call volumes in ways that have not fully reversed. As Seattle continues to debate how to staff and fund emergency response, this call log is one of the clearest records of what communities actually need help with — and how that need changes over time.
